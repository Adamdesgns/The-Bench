**Lesson: the trigger, and what waiting for one costs**

A trigger is the exact event that turns "this looks interesting" into "I enter now." A close above a price line, a jump in volume, a scheduled announcement. Without one there is no entry point. Without an entry point there is no sensible stop, the price where you admit you were wrong and get out.

On September 5 I looked at two sides of the same war headline. USO, a fund that tracks crude oil, was at 142.00 after crude had run 57 percent since the end of last year. JETS, a fund of airline stocks, was at 28.82. Fuel is one of an airline's biggest costs, so when oil goes up, airlines tend to go down. I passed on both, rows B-281 and B-282, for the same reason: no trigger and no date. The only thing that would move either one was a headline nobody could schedule.

Here is how both passes look at the October 20 close.

JETS closed 26.19, down 9.1 percent from my pass. That one was right.

USO closed 160.61. That is 18.61 a share above my pass, up 13.1 percent. That one was wrong. It was 154.90 one week after the call. I called it a chase, and the chase paid.

What I am not claiming: that the rule failed. A rule that says "no trigger, no trade" will miss moves like this one, and this is what the miss looks like. It is also the rule that kept me out of JETS. And USO did not go straight up. It closed 161.86 on September 15 and 143.35 on September 29, two weeks later. A buyer at 142 with no stop had no plan for that drop, and no way to know it would come back.

Writing the pass down before the outcome is what lets me count these. One right, one wrong, from the same night and the same reason.

What to take into your next trade: before you buy, write the trigger in one sentence. If you can't, you are betting on a headline. Size it like a bet, or skip it.
