**Lesson: a close is not a touch**

A trigger can be written two ways. "Touch" means the price trades at the level for any moment, even one second. "Close" means the price is still above the level when that day's session ends at 3:00 CT. A close trigger asks the market to hold the level for a whole day, not just pass through it.

On September 10 I had a plan on VRT, a company that sells power and cooling equipment to data centers. The rule, row B-322: no long until VRT closes above 258.49. The night before it had closed at 262.89, after falling from 290.83 the session before that.

VRT opened at 259.10 on September 10. That is above the line. It traded up to 262.00 in the first five minutes. By the end of those five minutes it was 250.68. Twenty-five minutes after the open it hit 244.77, the low of the day. It closed at 248.13.

If the rule had said touch, it would have fired at the open. A buyer at 259.10 was down 10.97 a share by the close. On 10 shares that is about 110 dollars gone on the first day, before any stop is counted. The close rule never fired, so nothing was bought.

It held up after that day too. VRT has not closed above 258.49 once since. The nearest it came was September 11: it traded up to 258.78 during the day, above the line, and closed at 257.06, under it. The same rule said no a second time. The plan expired unfired on September 18. VRT closed at 212.15 tonight.

What I am not claiming: that a close rule is always right. It costs you the first part of a real move, because you buy at the close instead of the first print. That is the price you pay for not being the person who bought the 259.10 open.

What to take into your next trade: if your plan says "a close above," then a price above the line during the day is not your signal, especially in the first minutes after the open. Wait for the session to end and look again.
