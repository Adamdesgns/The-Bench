**Lesson: the signal is not the entry**

A signal is the event that tells you a trade might be on. The entry is the price where you actually buy. They are two different moments. One rule I work under: a signal that shows up on a Friday close gets acted on Monday, and only if Monday's first 30 minutes of trading still sit at the planned price. If the price is gone by Monday, the trade is gone too.

On September 10 Oracle reported earnings. It closed that day at 152.94 and traded 159.50 after hours. The plan I wrote that night, row B-332: buy only between 149.70 and 149.90, with a stop at 139.50. A stop is the price where you admit you were wrong and sell. That buy range was only 20 cents wide because I wanted the target, 170.70, to pay at least twice what the stop could lose. Above 149.90 that math stopped working.

Friday, September 11, Oracle opened at 164.43, sold off all day and touched 149.84, inside the range. It closed at 150.28, row B-361. That was the signal. Monday's open was 141.42, row B-385. The range was already 8 dollars above the price. No trade.

Here is what the Friday buyer got. A buy at 149.84 with the stop at 139.50 was stopped out on September 16, when Oracle traded down to 139.00. That is about 10.34 a share lost, before any slippage, which means a stop can fill a bit lower than its price. Oracle closed at 125.54 tonight. A buyer with no stop at all is down 24.30 a share.

What I am not claiming: that the rule is free. Oracle bounced back to 152.00 on September 17 and 153.60 on September 22, above the old range. The rule had already killed the plan, so I did not chase it back up. That cost nothing this time, because it fell again, but a rule that waits will miss the times it doesn't.

What to take into your next trade: write the signal and the entry as two separate lines in your plan. When the signal fires, check the entry again before you buy. If the price has left your range, the plan is over, and you start a new one.
